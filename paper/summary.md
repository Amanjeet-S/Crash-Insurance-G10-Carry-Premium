# Crash insurance and the G10 carry premium

Amanjeet Singh. Extended abstract of the paper of 7 October 2026; the paper is [Crash_Insurance_and_the_G10_Carry_Premium.pdf](Crash_Insurance_and_the_G10_Carry_Premium.pdf) (source [main.tex](main.tex)).

This paper asks whether the price of crash insurance quoted in G10 currency option smiles accounts for the carry premium, and for its change between the zero-rate regime (May 2013 to December 2021) and the hiking regime that began in January 2022. I fixed the design and its interpretation rules on 23 September 2026, before computing any estimate. The design's question also names the collapse of the carry premium after 2008; because the one-month quotes that price 10Δ protection begin only in 2007 (Fenics) and between 2010 and 2013 (composite), the estimands compare the regimes from 2013, and the collapse appears only in the realised returns of the long at-the-money sample, 61.4 bp per month over 1995–2007 against 33.1 bp over 2009–2021.

I obtained the data from LSEG Workspace under a student licence provided by my university, which covers only me and does not permit redistribution of the raw data. The paper reports aggregate results, and the repository publishes month-level series at the level of the carry portfolio, transformed data that the licence holder confirmed may be published, from which the main portfolio-level results can be recomputed (E1 at 10Δ under the market reading, E2 with φ, E3 and its regime split, E5, the hedge-cost ratio, the long at-the-money sample apart from two details, and the comparison of the regime changes in the skew price and the spread); anyone rerunning the acquisition needs their own LSEG Workspace licence. Composite dealer quotes for nine currencies against the dollar are sampled at New York month-ends from May 2013 to August 2026, giving 160 month-ends and 159 return months. Fenics quotes from January 2007 (72 usable month-ends) serve as secondary evidence, and VIX futures settlements come from Cboe.

SABR smiles with β = 1 (Hagan et al., 2002) are calibrated to at-the-money, 25Δ risk-reversal and 25Δ market-strangle quotes (Reiswich and Wystup, 2012). Each leg of a three-long, three-short portfolio sorted on the forward discount (Lustig, Roussanov and Verdelhan, 2011) is protected by a one-month 10Δ option, following the hedged construction of Burnside et al. (2011). The primary estimand, φ, is the skew premium of that protection per unit of forward discount, compared across the two regimes. The other estimands ask whether φ predicts carry returns, split the realised hedge cost into payoff, volatility-level and skew terms, repeat the analysis under the other reading of the butterfly quote, and measure exposure to a VIX roll-down (Caballero and Doyle, 2012) and to global FX volatility (Menkhoff et al., 2012).

Under the pre-registered rules the answer is partial support only. The point estimate of φ falls from 0.809 to 0.454, but the difference of −0.355 has a Newey–West standard error of 0.204 and a bootstrap interval of [−0.814, 0.014], so I do not claim it. A supplementary split, added after the first results, shows that the skew cost of protection hardly moved (11.24 and 11.19 bp per month) while the forward-discount spread rose by 8.44 bp; realised carry returns were also higher after 2022 (37.0 against 7.4 bp per month), though not significantly so. φ predicts carry returns in sample (bias-corrected one-sided p = 0.021) but not out of sample, where the Clark–West statistic of 1.639 does not reach the 5% critical value of 1.645. The mean realised hedge cost is close to the skew premium paid ex ante (−11.2 bp per month), and it is so in both regimes: none of the payoff, volatility-level and skew terms differs between them. The hedge-cost ratio θ_UB is 0.53 against a diffusive null of about 0.10, but its confidence set is unbounded because the unhedged mean (17.7 bp, t = 1.47) is itself insignificant, and I do not read it as a crash share. The total hedge cost of −9.3 bp per month has t = −2.35 under the normal reference, but the option profit is strongly right-skewed and a studentised bootstrap interval, [−16.4, 1.7] bp, contains zero. Over 1995–2026 with at-the-money protection, a sample computed after the main results, the unhedged premium is significant (37.2 bp, t = 2.83) and the set is bounded, [0.27, 0.94], but it contains the at-the-money diffusive null of one half; bounding the ratio at 10Δ would take about 24 years of hedged returns at the primary sample's ratio of mean to volatility, and the quotes an out-of-the-money hedge needs begin in 2007. Compared with the literature, the 10Δ ratio of 0.53 sits above Jurek's (2014) bound of one third but its set contains that bound, and the at-the-money ratios of 0.45 and 0.56 are close to the ratio of about 0.47 implied by the at-the-money hedge of Burnside et al. (2011) and to the diffusive null of one half. Unhedged carry loads on the VIX roll-down (0.049, t = 7.41), and the hedge lowers that loading by about a fifth. Around 5 August 2024 the skew price of 10Δ protection rose from 11.2 to 19.4 bp per month in three business days and fell most of the way back within the month, while the carry spread hardly moved. Only a ten-currency ranking makes the fall in φ significant under both inferences, and there too it comes from the forward-discount spread. The evidence points to a wider carry spread rather than a change in the price of crash insurance.

Two post hoc analyses sharpen the answer. The skew price of protection did not change between the regimes (−0.05 bp, s.e. 1.30), nor did the oriented 10Δ risk reversal, while the forward-discount spread rose by 8.44 bp; the ratio of the two changes, Wald's (1940) grouping estimator, is −0.006 with a 95% test-inversion set of [−0.26, 0.49], and a one-for-one rise is rejected (t = −2.66). In the rare-disaster model of Farhi and Gabaix (2016), where risk reversals move with disaster exposure and inflation moves rates without a premium, this places the rise in the spread outside disaster exposure; the reading is mine and rests on treating the spread as an ex-ante premium. The second analysis asks how strong a tail assumption option-implied skewness needs when quotes stop at the 10Δ strikes. I show (theory result R10) that under the tail hypotheses of the moment bounds the quotes admit exponents only up to bounds set by the strike elasticities of the boundary prices, at which the tails are unique power laws, and I derive the sharp identified set of the moments. The boundary prices exclude the pre-registered boundary-elasticity setting in 75% of currency-months, and the sign of one-month skewness is identified only if the tail exponents are at least 63% of the admissible maximum in the median currency-month; Merton and Heston models with FX-typical parameters have exponents between 0.58 and 0.97 of the maximum, so identification depends on a strong and explicit tail assumption. I then tested that assumption out of sample with one-month 5Δ quotes of the Fenics contributor, available from June 2022, recording the test before retrieving their history. The 5Δ prices are consistent with the tail hypotheses in 99.1% of 450 currency-months, allow exponents of about 0.87 of the maximum in the median month, and, used as constraints, do not rule identification out: under the strongest hypothesis they allow, the sign of skewness is identified in 90.6% of the 446 consistent currency-months; they also show that the SABR smile extrapolated from 25Δ quotes overprices 5Δ protection by about 4%. One further price cannot confirm a hypothesis about a whole tail, so this is the favourable case.

The paper adds a decomposition of the realised cost of G10 crash insurance over 2013–2026, in each rate regime, to the option-hedged carry evidence of Jurek (2014), together with a pre-registered test of whether the skew price explains the change in the carry premium. The two readings of the butterfly quote, which Bossens et al. (2010) show can differ markedly for skewed smiles, move φ by under 4 per cent here. On the theory side, the currency-premium decomposition of Backus, Foresi and Telmer (2001), in the entropy form of Backus, Chernov and Zin (2014), is restated with the covered-interest-parity basis as a wedge, as Lustig and Verdelhan (2019) do with a general wedge. My own additions are bounds on option-implied moments from truncated quotes under a stated tail condition, which serve the purpose of the truncation bounds of Jiang and Tian (2005) under a different, stated tail condition and extend to the higher moment contracts; the characterisation of the tail exponents compatible with the quotes and the sharp identified set of the moments, which show that, for nonnegative weights, their upper bounds are attained and, for weights positive near the boundary, their lower bounds are not, with a check of the boundary-elasticity setting and a breakdown point for the sign of skewness (R10, with the known ingredients credited in the paper); an exact form with an error bound for the diffusive null of the hedge cost, whose leading-order form is due to Farhi et al. (2015); an explicit slope condition under which a smile delta determines a unique strike; and the joint smoothness and local well-posedness of the SABR calibration under FX delta conventions.

The hiking regime is short, with roughly 8 effective observations per regime in a post hoc calculation, and the cross-section has nine currencies. θ_UB is weakly identified, the realised terms depend on the crashes that happened to occur, and option-implied skewness is identified only under strong tail assumptions. The quotes are composite, payoffs use end-of-day spot rather than the 10:00 New York cut, dates follow New York holidays only, the revision check covers only the three days between my two retrievals, and the out-of-sample result depends on the training start. Requiring both intervals to exclude zero before a regime difference counts is my reading of the E1 rule.
