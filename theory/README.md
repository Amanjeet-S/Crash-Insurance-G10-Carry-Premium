## R1 (P\*). Domestic and foreign risk-neutral measures

Let (Ω, 𝔽, (𝔽_t), Q^d) satisfy the usual conditions, let B^d and B^f be strictly positive money-market accounts with B^d_0 = B^f_0 = 1, and let S be strictly positive and càdlàg. If Z_t = S_t B^f_t/(S_0 B^d_t) is a true Q^d-martingale, then dQ^f/dQ^d = Z_T defines a measure Q^f equivalent to Q^d, with E^{Q^f}[X | 𝔽_t] = E^{Q^d}[Z_T X | 𝔽_t] / Z_t for X ≥ 0 or X ∈ L¹(Q^f). Foreign prices discounted by B^f are then Q^f-martingales exactly when their domestic values discounted by B^d are Q^d-martingales, and under Garman–Kohlhagen E^{Q^d} S_T = F and E^{Q^f}(1/S_T) = 1/F, which resolves the paradox of Siegel (1972).

If Z is only a strict local martingale, then E Z_T < 1 and Z_T does not define an equivalent measure. Carr, Fisher and Ruf (2014) construct the foreign measure in that case as a Föllmer measure that is not equivalent to Q^d, and restore the domestic-foreign symmetry with a modified pricing operator. The result is the change of numeraire of Geman, El Karoui and Rochet (1995), with Girsanov's theorem and Itô's formula (standard statements, for example in Shreve, 2004).

## R2 (P\*). Arbitrage-free call prices

A function c satisfies c(K) = E(X − K)⁺ for some X ≥ 0 with E X = F if and only if c is convex, c(0) = F, c′₊(0) ≥ −1 and c(K) → 0 as K → ∞. The law of X is then unique, with distribution function G = 1 + c′₊ and P(X = 0) = 1 + c′₊(0); extending c by c(K) = F − K for K < 0, the law equals c″ in D′(ℝ). On a strike grid the tests used are slopes in [−1, 0] that are nondecreasing, and on total variance w(k) the condition g(k) ≥ 0 of Gatheral and Jacquier (2014); for finitely many strikes at one maturity, Davis and Hobson (2007, Theorem 3.1) give necessary and sufficient conditions of this form, with the zero-strike call included and prices strictly decreasing while positive. The link between call prices and state prices is due to Breeden and Litzenberger (1978), and the characterisation and its proof through the right derivative and Fubini's theorem are in Föllmer and Schied (2004, Lemma 7.23).

## R3 (P\*). Spanning

If f′ is locally absolutely continuous on (0, ∞), Q(X > 0) = 1 and ∫₀^F |f″|P dK + ∫_F^∞ |f″|C dK < ∞, then E f(X) = f(F) + ∫₀^F f″(K)P(K) dK + ∫_F^∞ f″(K)C(K) dK. The formula is that of Carr and Madan (2001), and the proof is the standard argument by Taylor's theorem with integral remainder and Fubini's theorem, written out with explicit integrability hypotheses. The moment contracts of Bakshi, Kapadia and Madan (2003) are of this form.

## R4 (P). Partial identification from truncated quotes

If Q(S_T ≤ K)/K^γ is nondecreasing on (0, K_min], then P(K) ≤ P(K_min)(K/K_min)^{γ+1} there, and if K^η Q(S_T > K) is nonincreasing on [K_max, ∞) with η > 1, then C(K) ≤ C(K_max)(K/K_max)^{1−η} there; for example, ∫₀^{K_min} P K⁻² dK ≤ P(K_min)/(γ K_min). The same inequalities bound the tails of any weight, including the moment contracts adapted from Bakshi, Kapadia and Madan (2003), with sign-changing weights split into positive and negative parts, so that variance and skewness are reported as intervals over stated tail exponents when the quotes stop at the 10Δ strikes. Between the quoted strikes the integrals use composite Simpson's rule with F as a node, because the integrand has a kink there; the trapezium error is O(h²) and the Simpson error O(h⁴) on each side (Süli and Mayers, 2003).

Jiang and Tian (2005, Proposition 2) bound the truncation error of model-free implied variance beyond the quoted strikes, and Lee (2004a) links finite moments to the wings of implied variance. The lemma is elementary, an instance of the monotone form of l'Hôpital's rule, and with an exact power-law tail its bounds hold with equality: this is the relative pricing rule of Taleb et al. (2023, Section II-A, eq. (3)) in the upper tail and the power-law put extrapolation of Benaim, Dodgson and Kainth (2008) in the lower. My own parts are the inequality under the weaker monotone-ratio hypotheses, the bounds for general and sign-changing weights and the resulting variance and skewness intervals; I have not found them in this form in the sources I consulted. The tail condition itself is a tail-restricted form of the star order against the power-function law and the Pareto law (Lando, Arab and Oliveira, 2023, Definition 2), as credited under R10. The implementation is `src/qef/fx/moments.py`, with R1 for USD-base pairs, and the results are in `reports/stage4.md`.

## R5. Delta-to-strike inversion

(a), marked P\*. For pips spot delta Δ = ω D_b Φ(ω d₊) of a call (ω = 1) or put (ω = −1) at a flat volatility σ, with D_b the base-currency discount factor, the strike has the closed form K = F exp(σ²τ/2 − ωσ√τ Φ⁻¹(|Δ|/D_b)), for Δ with the sign of ω and 0 < |Δ| < D_b. Reiswich and Wystup (2012; working-paper version of 20 March 2010, Sections 1.3 and 1.5, eqs. (15) and (16)) give the delta definitions and this closed form for spot and forward delta; the notes write out the inversion.

(b), marked P\*. The premium-adjusted call delta h(K) = (K/F) D_b Φ(d₋) tends to zero at both ends, and h′ has the sign of Φ(d₋) − φ(d₋)/(σ√τ). Since φ/Φ is strictly decreasing, h is strictly increasing and then strictly decreasing, with a unique maximiser K*, so each Δ in (0, h(K*)) has exactly two strikes, one on each side of K*, the maximum h(K*) is attained at K* only, and the convention takes the root in [K*, ∞).

(c), marked P\*. For pips delta Δ_ω(K) = ω D Φ(ω d₊(K, σ(K))), if |∂σ/∂k| √τ |d₋| < 1 on an interval, with k = ln(K/F), then Δ_ω is strictly decreasing there and the smile delta-to-strike map is injective. The condition holds over ±4 ATM standard deviations at every calibrated month-end, and premium-adjusted deltas are checked numerically.

Reiswich and Wystup (2012; working-paper version of 20 March 2010, Sections 1.3 and 1.5) give the delta definitions, the non-monotonicity of the premium-adjusted call delta, the right-branch convention, the bound by the simple delta and the first-order condition σ√τ Φ(d₋) = φ(d₋) for its maximum. Jäckel (2020, Section 2, eq. (20)) gives the single maximum through the inverse Mills ratio and the choice of the larger root, which is the argument written out for (b); that the inverse Mills ratio is decreasing follows from the log-concavity of Φ (Bagnoli and Bergstrom, 2005, Section 2.1, Theorem 1) and is classical (Sampford, 1953). Reiswich (2010, Section 3.8) shows that a smile delta can fail to be monotone. The condition in (c) is the two-sided form of the inequality of Fukasawa (2012, Lemma 2.1), which in this notation reads 1 + √τ d₋ ∂σ/∂k > 0 and holds for every arbitrage-free smile; his Proposition 2.1 obtains the monotonicity of d₊ from it by the derivative identity the notes use, and Mingone (2023, Lemma 4.1) shows that each delta has a unique strike if and only if d₊ is strictly decreasing along the smile and maps onto ℝ. Here the inequality is a hypothesis checked numerically on each fitted smile, which is not assumed to be free of arbitrage, much as Martini and Mingone (2022, Sections 3.1, 4.1 and 5.5.1) impose the same conditions on SVI slices. Nothing in (c) is mine beyond this check, for pips spot and forward deltas. The implementation is `src/qef/fx/gk.py`.

## R6 (P). Diffusive null of the hedge cost

Under Garman–Kohlhagen with equal P and Q volatility and no jumps, where the P-drift of log S exceeds the Q-drift by λ, θ₀ = 1 − μ_H/μ_U = Φ(−d₊(ξ)) exactly for some ξ between F and Fe^{λτ}, where d₊(G) = [ln(G/K) + s²/2]/s for the put's strike K and s = σ√τ, and |θ₀ − Φ(−d₁)| ≤ (2π)^{−1/2}|λ|√τ/σ with d₁ = d₊(F). The proof uses the mean value theorem with ∂_G p(G) = −Φ(−d₊(G)) for the undiscounted put. The leading-order form, that a position paired with a single put whose delta is not hedged, the construction of Burnside et al. (2011), earns 1 + Δ_P of the diffusive premium, is stated by Jurek (2014, Section 2.2 of the August 2013 version) and obtained as a small-maturity limit for their hedge by Farhi et al. (2015, Section 5.1, eqs. (8)–(9)); the mean-value form and the error bound, proved in the notes, are my own.

## R7 (P\*). Entropy decomposition

Let M, M* > 0 lie in L²(𝔽_{t+1}) with E_t|log M| < ∞ and E_t|log M*| < ∞, E_t M = e^{−r_t}, E_t M* = e^{−r*_t} and M S_{t+1}/S_t ∈ L². If one-period markets are complete in L², then M* = M S_{t+1}/S_t, and E_t[Δs + r* − r] = L_t(M) − L_t(M*) and E_t[s_{t+1} − f_t] = L_t(M) − L_t(M*) − x_t, where x_t = (f_t − s_t) − (r_t − r*_t) is the covered-interest-parity basis; with exchange rates quoted in foreign currency per USD, as in Du, Tepper and Verdelhan (2018), x_t is minus their basis. If the cumulant generating function of m has a Maclaurin series with radius of convergence greater than 1, then L_t(M) = Σ_{j≥2} κ_j/j!. For any gross return R > 0 with E_t[MR] = 1 and E_t|log R| < ∞, L_t(M) ≥ E_t log R − r_t; its unconditional form, the entropy bound E L_t(M) ≥ E[log R − log R_f] for funded returns R > 0, is as in Backus, Chernov and Zin (2014; eq. (4) of the working paper), who relate it to Bansal and Lehmann (1997) and Alvarez and Jermann (2005).

Backus, Foresi and Telmer (2001, Proposition 1 and eqs. (9)–(13)) give the decomposition of the currency premium into the difference between the two kernels' log-expectation and expectation-of-log terms, and its cumulant expansion. Backus, Chernov and Zin (2014) supply conditional entropy and the bound, Lustig and Verdelhan (2019) the decomposition with a wedge, and Jiang, Krishnamurthy and Lustig (2021) the convenience yield inferred from the Treasury basis; Brandt, Cochrane and Santa-Clara (2006) is cited for the remark on risk sharing. Writing the covered-interest-parity basis into the identity is an elementary step, derived in the notes.

## R8 (P). Local well-posedness of the smile calibration

The calibration solves three equations in (α, ρ, ν) at fixed β: the ATM delta-neutral-straddle volatility, the 25Δ risk reversal at the smile's own 25Δ strikes, and the equality of the market-strangle premium under the smile and under the flat volatility σ_ATM + BF. For β = 1 the implied volatility of Hagan, Kumar, Lesniewski and Woodward (2002) is infinitely differentiable on {K > 0} × (0, ∞) × (−1, 1) × [0, ∞) in (K, α, ρ, ν), which a lemma establishes. Suppose that at a solution: (i) the delta-neutral-straddle strike equation has a non-zero strike derivative, 1 ∓ Kσσ′(K)τ ≠ 0; (ii) the smile delta has a non-zero strike derivative at the smile's 25Δ call and put strikes, which for pips delta holds, for instance, under the condition of R5(c), while for premium-adjusted delta it must be checked directly, because a flat smile satisfies the R5(c) condition and the call delta's derivative still vanishes at K*; (iii) for premium-adjusted delta, the 25Δ call strike at the flat volatility σ_ATM + BF lies strictly to the right of the maximiser K* of R5(b), equivalently 0.25 < h(K*); and (iv) the Jacobian D_θΨ of the three equations in θ = (α, ρ, ν) is non-singular. Then the solution is locally unique and C¹ in the quotes, by the implicit function theorem applied first to the strikes and then to the system. Under the smile-strangle reading the result holds without (iii).

The implicit-function-theorem argument follows Reiswich (2010, Theorems 2 and 3, Appendices A and B), who uses it for the volatility-strike function and for the market-strangle calibration of a simplified parabolic smile. Le Floc'h and Kennedy (2014, Section 4, eqs. (30)–(31)) invert the leading-order expansion of the Hagan formula in closed form, recovering (α, ρ, ν) from the ATM level, skew and curvature, which is an approximate, global counterpart of R8. The three equations are the conditions of Reiswich and Wystup (2012, working-paper section on the construction of implied volatility smiles, eqs. (17), (18) and (25)), to which Reiswich (2010, Section 3.2) and, with β = 1, Clark (2011, Sections 3.7 and 3.10) calibrate SABR numerically, and Henrard (2011) applies the implicit function theorem to any calibration with as many instruments as parameters. In the lemma, the value of z/x(z) at z = 0 is that of the at-the-money formula of Hagan et al. (2002, eq. (2.18)), Choi and Wu (2021, eq. (5)) give its Taylor expansion there, and the integral representation is Hadamard's lemma (Milnor, 1963, Lemma 2.1). My own part is the explicit statement that the β = 1 formula is jointly smooth on the stated domain, including ν = 0, and the local well-posedness statement for SABR under conditions (i) to (iv), written for FX delta conventions; I have not found these in this form in the sources I consulted. The implementation is `src/qef/fx/smile.py`, in the variables (ln α, atanh ρ, ln ν).

## R9 (C). Inference

The central limit theorem for the sample mean holds under strict stationarity, E|X|^{2+δ} < ∞, Σ α(n)^{δ/(2+δ)} < ∞ and a positive long-run variance (Ibragimov, 1962, Theorem 1.7). The stationary bootstrap is that of Politis and Romano (1994), valid under the moment and mixing conditions of Theorem 2 of their 1991 technical report (Purdue Technical Report 91-03, the version consulted); Gonçalves and de Jong (2003) weaken the conditions. For the ratio θ, confidence sets of finite expected diameter have zero worst-case coverage (Gleser and Hwang, 1987), and so do sets that are bounded with probability one (Dufour, 1997), so test inversion is used; the set is Fieller's (1954, Sections 2–4) for a ratio of means with the variance estimator of Newey and West (1987), and it is bounded only when the denominator is significantly different from zero.

## R10 (P). Admissible tail exponents and sharp identification of moments

Under the hypotheses of R4, H_L(γ) (G(u)/u^γ nondecreasing on (0, K_min]) and H_U(η) (u^η Q(X > u) nonincreasing on [K_max, ∞)), with the boundary price and slope P0, G0, C0, Ḡ0 taken from the calibrated smile:

(a) some law compatible with (P0, G0) satisfies H_L(γ) if and only if γ ≤ γ̄ = K_min G0/P0 − 1, the strike elasticity of the put price minus one, and likewise η ≤ η̄ = 1 + K_max Ḡ0/C0; at the largest exponents the tails are unique power laws, so the moments are point-identified, and the hypotheses are nested in the exponents;
(b) writing G(K_min z) = G0 z^γ μ([0, z]) for a probability μ on [0, 1], the identified set of any finite vector of tail integrals is a compact convex set whose extreme points come from μ with at most two atoms, and the identified set of the raw moments is the Minkowski sum of the observed middle part and the two tail sets;
(c) for a nonnegative weight R4's upper bound is attained, by a power law below K_min with an atom at K_min (in the upper tail, by a Pareto law above K_max with an atom at K_max); its lower bound of zero is not sharp when, in addition, the weight is positive on a set of positive measure near the boundary, in (K_min z*, K_min), where K_min z* is the lowest point of the tail that attains the lower end (z* is fixed by P0, G0 and γ; likewise in the upper tail);
(d) with a density continuous at K_min, H_L(γ) implies γ ≤ γ_i, the boundary elasticity of setting (i); setting (i) holds exactly when the reversed hazard rate of ln X is smallest at the boundary, which a log-concave distribution function of ln X guarantees; and setting (i) requires γ_i ≤ γ̄, which, through K ε′ = ε(γ_i − γ̄) for the put elasticity ε = K P′/P, says that ln P is locally concave in ln K at K_min;
(e) the identified sets shrink as the fraction θ of the maximal exponents rises, so the fractions at which the sign of the skewness is identified form either the empty set or an interval with right end 1; in the second case its infimum θ* in [0, 1] is the breakdown point, and in the first, which occurs exactly when the third central moment at θ = 1 is zero, there is no breakdown point and the sign is identified at no fraction. This is proved without extra price constraints. With the 5Δ prices as equality constraints the identified sets are empty above the largest consistent fraction θ_5 and need not be a single point at θ_5, so (e) does not carry over; nesting gives only an interval with right end θ_5 or none, and the breakdown point θ*_5 is defined computationally, as the smallest fraction found by bisection along the common path below θ_5 at which the sign is identified. With one extra linear constraint per tail, the extreme points of (b) can have three atoms.

A consequence gives the identified interval of a price beyond a boundary in closed form, [max(0, P0 − G0 K_min (1 − k^{γ+1})/(γ+1)), P0 k^{γ+1}] for a put at K = k K_min, and its upper-tail counterpart; at γ = 0, where H_L holds for every law, it is the interval that convexity of the put price alone gives; the upper end is R4's bound, the ends move monotonically in the exponent, and the exponents at which an observed price is consistent with the hypothesis form an interval that starts at the weakest hypothesis and ends at a largest one, or are empty, as for at least one tail in the four USDJPY months that fail the test. This is the basis of the out-of-sample test with 5Δ quotes.

H_L and H_U are tail-restricted forms of the star order (Lando, Arab and Oliveira, 2023, Definition 2) against the power-function law and the Pareto law; for these reference laws the star order coincides with the reversed hazard rate order and the hazard rate order respectively, a one-line check of mine, and with a density H_L(γ) bounds the elasticity of the distribution function studied by Veres-Ferrer and Pavía (2022). The exponent K P′/P at the last strike is proposed as a natural power-law exponent by Benaim, Dodgson and Kainth (2008, footnote 6); Qin, Yang, Che and Feng (2026, Propositions 1 and 2) construct the power-law tails that match the boundary price and slope, whose exponents are exactly γ̄ and η̄ and are unique within the power-law family, and their Section 5.5 gives a necessary and sufficient condition for a shifted power-law right tail that also matches the boundary density, a condition of the same kind as, though different from, the check in (d); Figlewski (2010) attaches generalised extreme value tails to a recovered density inside the quoted range, matching the tail probability at one quantile and the density at two, and Bollinger, Melick and Thomas (2023) require such tails also to price the options at the outermost strikes, which (d) asks for H_L and H_U; Taleb et al. (2023, Section IV) derive from convexity at the anchor strike a restriction on a Pareto index, in their parametrisation a lower bound (eq. (10)), which is related to, but not the same as, the admissibility statement in (a); and Bollerslev, Todorov and Xu (2015, Section 3, eq. (3.3)) use an estimator of tail decay based on log-log slopes of out-of-the-money option prices. Sharp bounds on an option price given the prices of other options, without a tail hypothesis, go back to Bertsimas and Popescu (2002), and Lam and Mottet (2017) and Mottet and Lam (2017) obtain sharp bounds over shape-constrained tails whose boundary values are fixed by the observed part, by the device used here. The reduction to a moment problem over a mixing measure follows Popescu (2005, Section 3), the two-atom extreme points are Winkler's (1988, Theorem 2.1, as quoted by Pinelis, 2016, in the arXiv version of 2012), and the geometric description is Kemperman's (1968). Log-concavity is used through Bagnoli and Bergstrom (2005, Theorem 1). The breakdown point is the identification breakdown point of Horowitz and Manski (1995), as generalised by Stoye (2005, 2010) and set out by Masten and Poirier (2020), defined for the sign of skewness as Kline and Santos (2013, Example 2.3) define it for the sign of a parameter. Zhuang (2026) gives sharp bounds on option-implied tail quantities without a tail-shape hypothesis, and Bondarenko, Dillschneider, Schneider and Trojani (2026), according to their abstract, derive model-free bounds on the valuation uncertainty that an observed option cross-section leaves about a risk-neutral moment under market incompleteness, and find it unbounded for essentially all standard variance, skewness and kurtosis measures. The exponents γ̄ and η̄ are those of the matching power-law tails of Benaim et al. (2008) and Qin et al. (2026). My own parts are the characterisation of the admissible exponents under H_L and H_U, necessary and sufficient within the whole class, so that γ̄ and η̄ are the largest admissible exponents in the whole class, with uniqueness in the whole class at the maximum (not the observation that convexity restricts the index), the generating family with the boundary price and slope fixed, a device that Lam and Mottet (2017) and Mottet and Lam (2017) use for other shape classes, and the resulting identified set with its consequences for R4, the check of setting (i), and the breakdown analysis of option-implied skewness. The implementation is `src/qef/fx/identification.py`; the validation against Merton and Heston models is `tests/test_moments_models.py` and `reports/model_validation.md`.

## Supporting derivation: the Garman–Kohlhagen PDE (P\*)

The Garman–Kohlhagen price solves its PDE with terminal condition (S − K)⁺, which is verified directly (Garman and Kohlhagen, 1983; Shreve, 2004). It is the unique C^{1,2} solution of polynomial growth that is continuous up to T, by the Feynman–Kac argument with localisation, and no boundary condition at S = 0 is needed, because the exchange rate never reaches zero.
