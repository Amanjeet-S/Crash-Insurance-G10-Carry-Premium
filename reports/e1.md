## Construction

At each New York month-end the available currencies are ranked by the forward discount fd = log(X/F_X), with X in USD per unit of the currency, and the portfolio is long the three highest and short the three lowest; the extended sample uses two and two. A currency without the calibration set (spot, forward, rate, ATM, 25Δ risk reversal and 25Δ butterfly) is excluded that month, and a month is used only if at least twice the number of legs remain.

Each leg is protected by a one-month option that pays when the leg loses: a put on the currency, which is a USD call for USD-base pairs, struck at the 10Δ strike of the calibrated smile in the pair's delta convention (`src/qef/fx/crash.py` gives the mapping). V_smile and V_flat are the premia at the smile volatility and at the ATM volatility, in USD per USD of forward notional at delivery; for both quotation types this equals the undiscounted premium on the quoted pair divided by the pair forward. The estimand is φ = C_skew / FD, with C_skew = (1/n) Σ (V_smile − V_flat) and FD = (1/n)(Σ_long fd − Σ_short fd).

E1 uses the market-strangle reading and E4 the smile-strangle reading, and 25Δ hedges are a comparison. The ATM comparison is identically zero, because the calibrated smile passes through the ATM volatility. Spot, delivery and expiry dates are built on the New York business calendar (`src/qef/data/smile_inputs.py`), and volatility time is (expiry − trade)/365.

## Samples and inference

The primary sample uses composite quotes with three long and three short legs, from the primary start set by the audit rule to the last complete month. The extended sample uses Fenics quotes before the primary start, with two long and two short legs. The zero-rate regime runs from the primary start to December 2021 and the hiking regime from January 2022.

The pre-registered inference reports regime means with Newey and West (1987) standard errors, with the bandwidth chosen by the automatic procedure of Newey and West (1994) without prewhitening. The difference between the regimes is the coefficient on a regime dummy, with a Newey–West standard error, and its stationary-bootstrap percentile interval (Politis and Romano, 1994) resamples each regime separately with its own automatic block length (Politis and White, 2004; Patton, Politis and White, 2009), using 9,999 draws.

After the first results I added supplementary analyses, recorded in the research log on 24 September 2026; they do not replace the pre-registered estimand. They are the standard error of the difference across Newey–West lags from 0 to 20; an AR(1) plug-in standard error and the effective sample size per regime, T(1 − ρ)/(1 + ρ); medians, 10% trimmed means and the log-φ contrast; the ratio of regime means, mean C_skew over mean FD; separate regime differences for C_skew and FD; and the difference excluding 2020–2021 and excluding the largest zero-rate observations.

The splice test compares the skew cost of the long-side and short-side protective options leg by leg between Fenics and composite quotes, over every currency-month in which both have the calibration set, separately before and within the primary sample.

Under the pre-registered rules the regime difference in φ is not claimed, because both intervals include zero under both butterfly readings and both hedge deltas. The butterfly reading changes φ by about 4 per cent (E4 against E1). The C_skew and FD rows split φ into its numerator and denominator; that split was added after the first results and is therefore labelled post hoc.

The post hoc split shows that the skew price of crash insurance per month is essentially the same in the two regimes (difference −0.05 bp, s.e. 1.30), while the forward-discount spread is higher in the hiking regime (difference 8.44 bp, s.e. 2.30, interval [3.48, 13.65]). φ is highly persistent, with first-order autocorrelations of 0.85 and 0.76, which leaves roughly 8 effective observations per regime; an AR(1) plug-in standard error of the difference is 0.27 (t = −1.31). The Newey–West standard error of the difference rises with the lag, from 0.079 at lag 0 to 0.145 at 3, 0.180 at 6, 0.204 at 9, 0.220 at 12, 0.229 at 15 and 0.236 at 20, and the two-sided bootstrap p-value is 0.064.

Other location measures tell the same story. From the zero-rate to the hiking regime the median of φ falls from 0.53 to 0.38 and the 10% trimmed mean from 0.63 to 0.41; the mean of log φ falls from −0.46 to −0.89, a difference of −0.43; and the ratio of regime means, mean C_skew over mean FD, falls from 0.60 to 0.41. The difference depends on a few months. Excluding 2020–2021, the zero-rate months with the smallest forward discounts, it is −0.03 (80 zero-rate months), and dropping the largest 1, 3, 5 and 10 zero-rate observations gives −0.32, −0.28, −0.23 and −0.14.

Fenics and composite skew costs agree leg by leg in the splice test, with correlations of 0.995 before the primary sample and 0.997 within it. The extended sample, 72 Fenics month-ends from January 2007 to April 2013, has a mean C_skew of 22.3 bp and a mean φ of 0.64; because Fenics butterflies before 2010 are often stale, it is secondary evidence.

## Post hoc: where the rise in the carry spread came from

This analysis was added on 27 September 2026, after all pre-registered results were recorded, and is post hoc. It asks whether the rise in the forward-discount spread between the regimes was matched by a rise in the price of crash insurance, the question E1 answers through the ratio φ. Because φ is a ratio with a small and variable denominator, I compare the regime changes of its numerator and denominator directly.

```bash
.venv/bin/python scripts/estimate_regime_attribution.py
```

| Quantity (hiking minus zero-rate) | Difference | Newey–West s.e. | AR(1) plug-in s.e. | Bootstrap 95% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Skew price C_skew (bp per month) | −0.05 | 1.30 | 1.29 | [−2.44, 2.20] |
| Forward-discount spread FD (bp per month) | 8.44 | 2.30 | 3.01 | [3.48, 13.65] |
| Oriented 10Δ risk reversal (vol points) | 0.21 | 0.29 | 0.29 | [−0.34, 0.67] |

The oriented risk reversal is the quoted volatility of the protective option less that of the opposite option, averaged over the six legs (55 hiking months, because one return window is incomplete). The rise in the spread survives the persistence-robust AR(1) standard error (t = 2.80), and neither measure of the price of crash protection changed.

The ratio of the two regime differences, ΔC_skew/ΔFD = −0.006, is the grouping estimator of Wald (1940) with the regime as the grouping variable. Its 95% set, found by test inversion as for θ_UB (Fieller, 1954; with one binary instrument this is the set of Anderson and Rubin, 1949), is [−0.26, 0.49] with Newey–West standard errors and [−0.23, 0.48] with the stationary bootstrap resampling the pair (C_skew, FD) jointly within each regime. It is bounded because ΔFD is significant. At most about half of the rise in the spread was matched by a rise in the skew price, and the hypothesis that the skew price rose one for one with the spread is rejected (difference −8.49 bp, Newey–West t = −2.66, two-sided bootstrap p = 0.009).

In the rare-disaster model of Farhi and Gabaix (2016), a currency pair's risk reversal is proportional to the difference in the two countries' resilience (Lemma 2, eq. (34)), the part of the real interest differential that compensates for disaster risk moves with that difference (Proposition 3, eq. (22)), and the expected carry return is proportional to it (Proposition 4, eqs. (24)–(25)), whereas inflation moves nominal rates without moving risk reversals or risk premia (proof of Proposition 9). Reading these equations for a change between regimes, which they do not discuss, an unchanged risk reversal and skew price with a wider spread means that the rise did not come from disaster exposure; in their model it came from the component of the differential that carries no premium, so the expected carry return need not have risen. This reading is mine. It rests on the random-walk interpretation of the spread as an ex-ante premium that underlies φ, under which carry equals the expected return only if the expected spot change is zero (Koijen, Moskowitz, Pedersen and Vrugt, 2018, eqs. (4)–(5)), and which disaster models reject. The evidence adds the 2013–2026 period to earlier findings of a weak within-currency relation between option skew and interest differentials (Brunnermeier, Nagel and Pedersen, 2008, Table 4 of NBER Working Paper 14473; Jurek, 2014, footnote 12 of the SSRN version of August 2013), against which Farhi et al. (2015, Table 5 of the SSRN version of March 2015) find a significant relation for option-implied disaster exposure.

## Design checks computed after the main results

The design names two checks of E1's inputs that were computed only on 27 September 2026, after the results above had been recorded. `scripts/estimate_design_checks.py` states their rules; an independent review of the script preceded this record.

SOFR OIS in place of Fed Funds OIS. The dollar rate enters E1 only through the base-currency discount factor D_b = F D_q/S of the four USD-quoted pairs (EUR, GBP, AUD and NZD); for the dollar-base pairs no dollar rate enters, and FD depends only on spot and forward. One-month SOFR OIS is available under the month-end rule at every month-end from June 2018 to August 2026 (99 month-ends). Over that window SOFR OIS less Fed Funds OIS averages −0.26 bp a year (s.d. 4.28, largest absolute difference 14.0 bp). I recalibrated the 396 affected smiles with SOFR discounting through the same call path as the published calibration, which reproduces the stored smiles to 5.3e-12 when run with Fed Funds; all converged and pass both arbitrage checks. D_b changes by at most 0.013%, φ by at most 1.1e-4 and C_skew by at most 0.002 bp in any month. The regime difference in φ over the whole primary sample, with SOFR spliced in from June 2018, is −0.3548 (Newey–West s.e. 0.2039; bootstrap 95% [−0.8136, 0.0142]) under both rates, and no conclusion changes in sign, Newey–West interval or bootstrap interval for φ, C_skew or FD. Over the SOFR window alone the φ difference is −0.79 and both intervals exclude zero under both rates; that window is set by the availability of SOFR, its "zero-rate" part is 2018 to 2021, and it is not a test of E1.

The named-broker contributor. Of the named-broker contributors in the panel, one quotes EUR one-month ATM volatility, 25Δ risk reversal and 25Δ butterfly over a useful span, from July 2018; it quotes no 10Δ strikes. Under the month-end rule, over 96 month-ends, its ATM mid is on average 0.018 volatility points below the composite (s.d. 0.086) and 0.025 below Fenics, and its 25Δ risk reversal and butterfly differ from the composite by +0.003 and +0.004 points on average; 47 of its 82 butterfly month-ends are substituted, and its constant spreads (0.25 and 0.30 points) suggest indicative quotes. SABR smiles calibrated to its quotes with composite spot, forward and rates (82 month-ends, all converged and arbitrage-free) give a 10Δ skew cost of the EUR protective option 0.068 bp per month above the composite's on a mean of 2.70 bp (s.d. of the difference 0.32; correlation 0.994); Fenics differs from the composite by −0.051 bp. Replacing the EUR leg's contribution at the 56 E1 month-ends where EUR is a leg and the broker smile exists (EUR is short in all of them) changes C_skew by +0.028 bp on average on 11.47 bp and φ by +0.003 on 0.92. Because neither the broker nor the composite quotes the 10Δ volatility used for the leg here (the composite's 10Δ quotes are held out), the check compares two extrapolations from 25Δ quotes.
